# Starting my co-op training at Aiqona as a game developer

I am starting my co-op training at Aiqona as a game developer. This is an important step for me because it moves my Unity experience from mostly self-directed projects, game jams, and tools into a professional team environment.

I have built and shipped projects before, but working inside a company introduces a different standard: the code must serve a shared product, decisions must be communicated, and progress must remain understandable to people beyond the person who wrote the feature.

> **What I want from this experience**
> - Learn how a professional game-development team plans and reviews work.
> - Improve how I communicate technical decisions and respond to feedback.
> - Contribute reliable Unity and C# work without losing sight of the player experience.

![Notebook-style illustration marking the start of co-op training at Aiqona](images/blog/aiqona-coop.svg)

## Moving from personal ownership to shared ownership

In a personal project, I can keep a large amount of context in my head. I know why a system exists, which shortcuts I accepted, and where the fragile parts are. A team cannot depend on that invisible context.

During this training, I want to get better at making work easier for other developers to understand and extend. That means clearer task breakdowns, smaller focused changes, useful documentation, and code reviews treated as part of development rather than as a final gate.

Shared ownership also means learning when to defend a technical decision and when to change it. The goal is not to prove that my first solution was correct. The goal is to help the team ship the strongest result.

## Applying my Unity experience in a production setting

My background is strongest in Unity and C#. I have worked on gameplay systems, custom editor tools, UI, game-jam projects, and packages for other developers. Co-op training gives me the opportunity to apply that foundation within real production constraints.

I want to pay close attention to the full path a feature takes: understanding the requirement, identifying dependencies, implementing it, testing edge cases, receiving feedback, and confirming that it works in the intended build. A feature is not complete just because it behaves correctly once in the Editor.

That production mindset is one of the most valuable things I can develop during the placement.

## The areas I want to strengthen

My priorities are practical:

- Write maintainable gameplay code that fits an existing codebase.
- Estimate tasks more accurately and surface risks early.
- Test changes across the states and inputs that can break them.
- Communicate progress clearly without hiding uncertainty.
- Understand how design, art, programming, and QA affect the same feature.

I also want to learn the team's everyday habits: how work is scoped, how feedback is recorded, how bugs are reproduced, and how a feature moves from an idea to something players can use.

## What I will keep documenting

I plan to use this blog to record the lessons that can be shared responsibly: technical patterns, workflow improvements, mistakes that changed how I think, and the difference between building alone and contributing inside a team.

I will keep company and project details private when they are not mine to share. The useful part of a lesson can usually be explained without exposing confidential work.

Starting at Aiqona is not the end of learning how to become a game developer. It is the point where that learning becomes more collaborative, more accountable, and closer to the way games are actually made.
